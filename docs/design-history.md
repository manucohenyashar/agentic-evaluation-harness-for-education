## What changed in versions 2 through 2.7

Version 1 recommended a "reflect-and-revise" calibration loop: score a handful of teacher-graded samples, find where the system disagreed with the teacher, and rewrite the rubric wording to close the gap. That recommendation had a hole in it. If the human teacher graded against the *original* rubric, and the system then rewrites that rubric, the human scores are no longer strictly comparable to anything the revised system produces. And since re-grading is not something you can ask a working teacher to do, the loop appeared to have no valid anchor.

The hole is real, and version 1 also understated the sample size the technique requires. Both are fixed here:

- **Section 6 is new.** It is the full treatment of how to calibrate a rubric without asking anyone to re-grade, including six concrete guardrails against the specific failure mode where an optimizer reproduces a teacher's numbers while quietly abandoning the teacher's standards.
- **Section 5.7 is rewritten** and now points at Section 6 rather than recommending naive optimization.
- **Stage B of the architecture is redesigned** from "revise the rubric to match the scores" to "discover ambiguities and let the teacher author the resolution."
- **Stage D is promoted** from a review screen to the system's primary validation instrument.
- **Section 3.5 and Section 3.6 are new**, covering small-sample statistical instability and construct validity in plain language.
- **Sample-size guidance is corrected.** Version 1 said 5 to 10 calibration samples. That number is fine for finding ambiguities and far too small to validate anything. Sections 3.5 and 6.6 explain the distinction.

**Version 2.1** adds two further corrections. First, version 2 said to score "one criterion per call," which is necessary but not sufficient: in an agent framework a call can still carry conversation history, and the contamination that matters comes from shared *context*, not from shared processes. Section 7.2 is new and specifies judgment isolation properly, as three rules covering context isolation, declared criterion dependencies, and a read-only hierarchical synthesis pass. Second, Section 8.4 is new and specifies the execution plan: the loop nesting that maximizes prefix-cache reuse, the prompt-ordering rule it forces, why criterion-batching is a fairness property rather than only a speed one, and the on-disk **working store** that lets criterion dependencies resolve across batches without ever passing a score. Section 6.2's schema lock now covers the dependency graph, Stage C of the architecture is redrawn as two sweeps around the store, and checkpointing and progress reporting are respecified at cell rather than student granularity.

Everything else (the research base, the panel composition, the local-inference stack) carries forward unchanged.

**Version 2.7.** Adds Section 5.10, the scoring scale: judges emit a **behaviourally-anchored band label**, never a number, and the numeric points are derived afterwards by the orchestrator. Closes a central-tendency bias the design was fully exposed to and that none of its metrics could see. Requirements R39 to R44.

**Version 2.6.** Adds Section 7.7, the ingestion and transcription module: PDF in, structured Markdown out, via a vision-language model, with the validation ladder that catches missing pages and mismatched submissions before they can be scored. Requirements R32 to R38.

**Version 2.6** establishes the invariant that **evaluation happens against Markdown and never against a PDF** (R38), and specifies the ingestion module that enforces it — which every prior version treated as a box labelled "OCR if needed" while simultaneously identifying it (§0.2) as a real subsystem and (§8.5) as the acceptance-test line most likely to fail. Section 7.7 is new and specifies it concretely: PDFs of mostly handwritten work in, one **structured Markdown artifact** out, transcribed page by page by a vision-language model (Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct as the reference choice), with deterministic assembly when a single document arrives as several PDFs. Two consequences justify the section's length. First, that Markdown artifact is the **canonical source text** — every evidence span the panel cites is a byte offset into it and §7.4's integrity gate verifies those offsets against it — so it has to be immutable, content-hashed, and versioned, which nothing previously said. Second, a **five-gate validation ladder** runs before scoring, and gate V4 addresses a failure mode this design had no defense against at all: a student's answer sheet submitted against the wrong test. Every criterion then legitimately finds no evidence, the panel unanimously scores zero, and §7.4's confidence inversion does not fire because the evidence is not corrupt — it is genuinely absent. A student who did the work correctly receives a confident, unanimous zero. That is the most damaging silent failure in the system and it is cheap to catch at ingest and impossible to catch later. Requirements R32 to R38 are added; §8.4's throughput arithmetic now models the OCR pass it previously excluded, and it is not small. The section is also explicit that the transcription model and the evaluation panel are **independent choices on unrelated criteria** — the panel will in practice be stronger, more carefully bias-validated models, because judging a partial-credit response is the harder task, and a vision model reading handwriting well is no evidence either way about how it would judge.

**Version 2.5** makes **cloud hosting and OpenRouter-served inference first-class requirements** rather than something the architecture forbade. Versions 2.0 to 2.4 wrote the disconnected school as the only deployment and R1 as a blanket prohibition on cloud inference. That over-generalized a correct argument: what a rarely-exercised path being unvalidated actually forbids is *runtime failover between backends*, not the existence of a second backend. Two facts force the correction. The system is developed and tested in Linux containers on Windows hosts with every model call going through OpenRouter — a Mac-only, Ollama-only design is a design that cannot be tested — and a large share of real deployments (connected schools, district instances, programs with no capital budget) are better served hosted than by shipping hardware. Section 0.7 is new and defines three deployment profiles (`edge-local`, `cloud-hosted`, `dev-ci`), the provider abstraction that makes them one code path (R27), and the rule that replaces the old prohibition: **the backend is fixed by configuration before a run starts and never changes mid-run**, because a run whose criteria were scored partly by a 4-bit local quantization and partly by a hosted build is a run whose statistics mean nothing. Section 8.7 is new and specifies the OpenRouter path in full — model pinning against provider-side build substitution, determinism limits, cost ceilings, rate-limit behavior, and the backend conformance suite. Requirements R27 to R31 are added; R1, R4, and R5 are rescoped from global rules to profile-scoped ones. The correction has a sting in the tail that Section 0.7 states explicitly: because CI runs on OpenRouter continuously, the **local** path now becomes the under-exercised one, and it is the path deployed to schools with no IT support — so the Section 8.5 hardware acceptance test is reaffirmed as an edge-profile gate that a cloud run cannot satisfy.

**Version 2.4** responds to three independent adversarial reviews of the v2.3 design and adds requirements R19 to R26. Two findings were serious enough to change the architecture. First, **the extraction stage was an undefended common-mode failure**: judges were independent of each other but all consumed one extractor's output, so an extraction error produced unanimous agreement and *raised* confidence, meaning the system was most certain exactly when it was systematically wrong. Section 7.4 adds an evidence-integrity gate and inverts the confidence calculation. Second, **teacher acceptance was being treated as ground truth**, which automation bias makes progressively less true as the system improves; Section 6.8 now separates operational labels from blind gold labels and requires a small blind sample per administration. Also added: escalation policy rebuilt on observable signals rather than model self-confidence, with circuit breakers for escalation spikes (§7.1); enforced hardware profiles including single-model-in-VRAM for consumer GPUs, plus a prefix token budget (§8.1, §8.4); a hardware acceptance gate replacing the claim that R10 was satisfied (§8.5); OCR routing on score impact rather than confidence, with structured templates and cross-cohort transcription clustering (§7.5); a provisional-score policy for the review residual (§10); population-scoped package validation as a schema constraint (§9.5); separate metrics for feedback, scores, and diagnosis (§10); and a revised exemplar policy after review showed synthetic-by-default sacrifices the construct fidelity anchors exist to provide (§9.4).

**Version 2.3** adds Section 9, the memory and persistence layer, built around three jobs rather than one: surviving multi-hour runs, **reusing a tuned assessment across cohorts without retuning**, and accumulating validation across administrations. The central new object is the **Assessment Package** (Section 9.4), a portable versioned bundle of test, calibrated rubric, dependency graph, exemplars, validated panel config, and cumulative validation record, which a teacher loads to grade a new cohort immediately. Packages appreciate with use: each administration adds labels and tightens the recorded agreement statistics. Section 9 also specifies four lifetime tiers with full DDL, the JSON exchange contracts between orchestrator and workers, per-cohort and per-package statistics, a content-hash work ledger for resumability, a failure taxonomy, and an infrastructure recommendation (SQLite at the school node, Postgres only for a central catalog, agent-memory frameworks not at all). Its governing rule is that **the orchestrator reads memory and the judges never do**: this is a persistence layer, not an agent memory, because a store a judge could query at inference time would reopen every contamination path Section 7.2 closes.

**Version 2.2** adds Section 0, the problem statement, and re-derives several decisions for the real deployment scale. Earlier versions implicitly assumed a 30-student class. The target classes run **150 to 350 students**, which is a different engineering problem: roughly 23,000 model calls per assessment rather than 2,000. Three things change materially. A uniform three-judge panel becomes the dominant cost, so Section 7.1 adds **adaptive panel depth**, escalating to the full panel only where the research says disagreement lives. Serving-stack choice becomes decisive rather than advisory, because single-stream inference turns a two-hour job into a 38-hour one; Section 8.4 works the arithmetic. And a fixed-percentage human review queue becomes unusable at ~790 flagged items, so Section 10 replaces it with a **teacher-minute budget ranked by expected value** (R12). Power is stable via solar in the target sites, so resumability is now justified by run duration rather than by outages. One consequence runs the other way and is worth noting: at n=350 the Stage D override log accumulates hundreds of labels per assessment, so the validation regime in Section 6.8 is reached in one or two assessments rather than over a semester.
